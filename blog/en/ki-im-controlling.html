AI in controlling: the month-end report that explains itself

24 June 2026 · 2 min read · Maris Reinold

In controlling the work rarely sits in the calculating. The numbers are often ready early in the month. What takes time is everything around them: finding the variances, gathering the causes, writing the report, briefing management clearly. “The numbers are done” turns into “the commentary is done” — and a week of grind lies in between.

That grind is a rewarding AI case. Not because AI would do controlling better than an experienced human. But because it takes over the dull part and frees the mind for the judgement.

Where AI really helps in controlling

  • Finding variances: which cost centre, account or product falls out of the ordinary this month? The AI works through the numbers and puts the anomalies in front of you — instead of you clicking through thirty rows.
  • Drafting commentary: “Material costs 12 percent over plan, driven by item X.” The AI writes the first draft of the commentary in seconds. You check it, add what only you know, and approve.
  • Preparing reports: from the same numbers, the report for management, one for the bank, one for the team — each in the right tone and level of detail. Numbers once, several audiences.
  • Answering questions: “Why did the margin in the South region drop?” Instead of building a new analysis, you ask in words — and get the answer with the numbers behind it.

What the AI doesn't take over

The judgement stays with the human. The AI spots that material costs rose — whether that's a one-off, a supplier problem or the start of a trend, you know. It delivers the draft, you deliver the interpretation. And anything that goes outside or feeds a decision belongs proofread. An AI sounds confident even when it has mixed up a number.

The prerequisite no one likes

For this to work, the AI has to reach the numbers — clean and current. If the data sits in five Excel islands with their own logic, AI won't help either. Data base first, then automation. That's the uncomfortable but honest order — and often the real first step.

Start small — here too

You don't have to automate the whole close. Take one building block that grates every month:

  • Which analysis do you rebuild by hand every month?
  • Which comment is 80 percent the same every time?
  • Which question does management ask every time — and you search anew every time?

One of these blocks, solved cleanly, saves visible time next month. And one block becomes, step by step, a close that largely explains itself — while you handle what you're really needed for: the judgement.
