AI in financial planning: forecasts that are more than rolled-over Excel

25 June 2026 · 2 min read · Maris Reinold

Most financial plans in mid-sized companies share a fate: they're outdated the day they're finished. A big order slips, a customer pays 30 days late, an investment is pulled forward — and the neat spreadsheet no longer holds. It still gets updated only once a quarter, because the upkeep is so tedious.

Here AI is less magic than diligent help — and that's exactly what makes it valuable. It takes off the planning what makes it a slog: the constant manual catching-up.

Where AI comes in for financial planning

  • Entering actuals: what actually flowed, the AI pulls from the systems and reconciles with the plan — instead of someone copying bank statements into Excel.
  • Rolling the forecast forward: based on past payment behaviour, it estimates when open items will realistically come in — not by payment term, but by actual behaviour.
  • Running scenarios: “What if the major customer pays 30 days later?” “What if we buy the machine in Q3 after all?” Answers in minutes instead of a worked-through spreadsheet.
  • Flagging liquidity gaps early: instead of being startled by the account balance, you get the heads-up when things could get tight in six weeks — with time to act.

The decisive difference: continuous instead of one-off

The real gain isn't the single analysis but the rhythm. A plan that updates itself isn't right once a quarter but every week. A document that ages becomes a picture that keeps pace. That changes how early you can make decisions — and with liquidity, early is almost everything.

Staying honest: AI doesn't predict the future

A forecast stays an estimate. AI makes it more current and faster, not clairvoyant. A one-off special effect, a market shock, a default unlike any before — no model catches that reliably. So: the AI delivers the basis, you check the assumptions. And for anything you put before the bank or the board, the human stays responsible. A fast number is only worth something if someone has understood it.

Where to start

  • Which plan do you update less often than you should — because it's too tedious?
  • Which “what if” question do you ask yourself often without properly working it through?
  • Are the needed numbers already in a system, or would you have to hunt them down first?

Even a liquidity outlook that updates itself weekly changes the feeling in your gut — away from reacting, towards looking ahead.
