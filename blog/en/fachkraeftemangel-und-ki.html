Skilled-worker shortage and AI: no replacement, but a lever

20 July 2026 · 2 min read · Maris Reinold

There's a lot of talk about AI and jobs — usually in the fear version: “The machine takes our jobs away.” In the reality of smaller companies the problem is the opposite. It's not that there are too many people for the work, but too few.

The situation in numbers

According to the ifo Institute, around a fifth of companies currently report a shortage of skilled workers — somewhat less in industry, somewhat more in mechanical engineering. The good news in it: the figure has recently fallen slightly, to the lowest level in years. The bad news: forecasts see areas like metal, machinery and electrical continuing to lack hundreds of thousands of skilled workers over the coming years. The gap remains.

Why “replacement” is the wrong question

If hands are missing anyway, the question isn't “how do I replace people with AI?” but “how do I make sure the scarce skilled workers are free for what matters?”. That's a completely different view — and the more productive one. AI replaces no one here, it eases the load.

Where the relief gets concrete

  • Taking over routine: documentation, standard enquiries, gathering information — tasks that cost skilled workers time without demanding their skill.
  • Sharing knowledge: when experienced colleagues are missing, AI helps make their knowledge findable — for those who are new or have to step in.
  • Speeding up onboarding: new people find answers themselves faster, instead of tying up one of the few experienced staff for every little thing.

Staying honest

AI doesn't solve the skilled-worker shortage. A good specialist at the machine, in the customer conversation, in the difficult decision stays irreplaceable — and becomes more valuable, not less. What AI can do: make sure those people spend their time where it counts, instead of seeping away into routine. That's no miracle promise, but a real lever.

For many businesses that's even the most convincing way into the topic: not “save on staff”, but “keep and unburden the good people”.
