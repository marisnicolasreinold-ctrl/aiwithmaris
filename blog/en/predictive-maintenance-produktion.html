Predictive maintenance: foreseeing machine failures before they get expensive

7 July 2026 · 2 min read · Maris Reinold

A machine stoppage never comes at a good time. But the most expensive one is the one that surprises you — mid-shift, mid-order, when the spare part has to be ordered first and the line stands still. That surprise is exactly what predictive maintenance sets out to abolish.

The idea is simple: don't wait until something breaks. Don't stubbornly replace on schedule either, whether needed or not. Instead, let the machine tell you when it needs attention.

How it works

Sensors deliver data continuously — vibration, temperature, power draw, sound. An AI learns what “normal” sounds like and flags it when a pattern shifts that a human would miss. A bearing that will fail in three weeks often announces itself long beforehand — in signals too subtle to notice in daily operation.

What the numbers say — and what they don't

Industry analyses report around 20 to 40 percent fewer unplanned stoppages where predictive maintenance runs cleanly. Such ranges are a good guide, but not a promise: what you get depends on your machines, your data situation and the implementation. What stays honest — the leverage is real, the concrete number you have to measure yourself.

The prerequisite that's often missing

No data, no prediction. Older machines without sensors have to be retrofitted first, and the signals have to come together somewhere. That's effort — but a one-off that pays interest with every stoppage avoided. And without clean data the system warns about the wrong things.

Start small — here too

  • Which single machine hurts most when it stops unplanned?
  • Does it already deliver data — or would sensors be the first step?
  • What does an hour of downtime on that exact line cost you today?

Start with the most expensive, most failure-prone machine, not the whole hall. One proven case on one line convinces more than any glossy presentation — and carries the decision for the next.
